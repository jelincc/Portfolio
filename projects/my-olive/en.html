Purpose · Hypothesis

Launched as a New Product With Improved Sound Quality and Amplification Over Gen 1 and 2

Objective

Expand the domestic and global smart hearing aid market · grow active users to support a data business · raise awareness around hearing loss prevention

Hardware

Built-in voice amplification covering moderate hearing loss, plus data accumulation through ambient noise collection

Software

Optimization tuned to a 5-minute hearing test, a hearing-prevention report, situational modes and detailed settings, and personalized information based on test results

Shifting perception was the core challenge: The project started from redefining hearing aids — from "a product only for seniors" to "an essential for hearing care."

If:

we design the hearing aid not as a "specialized medical device" but as a "software product for managing your hearing,"

Then:

the barrier to entry drops, and more users will comfortably buy and use it.

Define

Three Core Problems and Their Response Strategies

Hearing Loss Prevention

Problem: excessive exposure to ambient noise

Recommend a personalized adaptation program by hearing loss type and severity, and provide real-time noise measurement reports and noise alerts

Convenience

Problem: testing and setup only possible through a professional visit

Provide a mode/volume/detailed settings UI, plus device connection and cleaning manual videos

Ongoing Care & Peace of Mind

Problem: condition diagnosis only possible through a visit

Friendly-toned prevention notifications, plus immediate alerts for excessive noise or device issues

From Easy Mode to Hearing Care, Core Values Mapped to Screens

From Easy to Use, to real-time noise monitoring, to hearing health management, to personalized hearing profiles — here's how My Olive's core features break down screen by screen.

Main Page

Control mode and volume settings right from the home screen

The early app placed the hearing report on the home screen, but we changed the home screen to a control screen instead, matching what users actually came to do most: adjust controls.

The top area shows the current mode's values as a graph based on the hearing test, while the bottom lets users adjust mode, volume, and detailed EQ.

Boost

The mode that amplifies voices to the max. Used for conversations, work meetings, watching TV, and similar situations.

Clear

The mode closest to natural hearing. Used in everyday settings, outside of any special situation.

Quiet

The mode that blocks out disruptive ambient noise. Used when you want to shut out noise and settle into calm.

Hearing Report

A hearing-prevention report that measures ambient noise levels in real time. It measures all noise collected through the device and played through the phone speaker, accumulates it into a report, and warns users with an alert once levels reach a point that could cause hearing damage.

Training

A personalized adaptation program to help users get used to the device. For users who struggle to adjust to amplified sound, we offer an individualized 9-month program, using hearing-related articles and simple quizzes to keep it as engaging as possible.

Usability Testing

All 3 Hypotheses Rejected: "More Explanation Builds Trust" Was Wrong

We designed the usability test by pulling together GA data, CS inquiries, and country-level survey findings. We recruited 7 male participants in their 50s–70s across four remote/app usage-experience types (A-1, A-2, B, C, spanning our own and competitor products) and ran interviews with each.

Hypothesis ①

Providing a hearing-prevention report will make users more aware of hearing loss risk and feel they need the product.

Hypothesis rejected

Surprisingly, users didn't feel much concern about hearing prevention, and the report wasn't a meaningful factor in their purchase decision.

Hypothesis ②

Spelling out every screen and feature in detail will make it easier for older users to understand the service.

Hypothesis rejected

We provided a detailed, spelled-out tutorial, but over 80% of participants still said the product wasn't explained enough.

Hypothesis ③

A dashboard-style control screen with plenty of detailed settings will feel professional, build trust, and let users customize comfortably.

Hypothesis rejected

Over 90% of users wanted simple, function-first controls rather than a visual dashboard, and most only touched the detailed options during initial setup — after that, they relied solely on preset buttons.

Key insight: The assumption that "more explanation and more options build trust and comfort" turned out wrong on all three counts. The older the user, the more they judged trust by how simple the controls were — not by how much information was on offer. Even before the usability test, the control screen already made up over a third of the whole app, and we redesigned both the Bluetooth pairing flow (originally 6 steps) and the 14-step tutorial around this same principle.
Decisions & Trade-offs

Control Screen as the Main Hub, Report and Training Below It

The early app placed the hearing report on the home screen, but GA data showed that "control" was actually the screen users relied on most — so we restructured the main screen around control.

Main Page · Control

The top shows the current mode as a graph based on the hearing test; the bottom handles mode, volume, and detailed EQ. Three modes — Boost (max amplification), Clear (near-natural default), Quiet (noise blocking) — plus a Noise Reduction slider.

Hearing Report

Measures both device-collected ambient noise and phone-speaker playback noise, accumulating them into a report. Sends a warning alert once noise reaches a level that could cause hearing damage.

Training

A 9-month personalized adaptation program for users who struggle to adjust to amplified sound. Built around hearing-related articles and quizzes so unfamiliar information stays approachable.

As-Is → To-Be Redesign Wireframes

We redesigned the graph-heavy dashboard screen into one centered on situational presets — TV, Lecture, Outside, Restaurant, and so on — simplifying control down to just picking the right preset instead of digging into detailed settings.

01Restructure the home screen from "report-centered" to "control-centered"
RiskThe report was originally on the home screen specifically to keep reminding users about hearing-loss risk, and demoting it to a sub-page could weaken that purpose.
ResponseSince GA confirmed control was the #1 real usage priority, we moved it to the main screen — but kept the report's role (raising awareness) intact by sending a separate warning alert whenever the noise threshold was crossed.
02Sequence development by country/language team after defining the MVP
RiskThe software scope — hearing tests, training, reports — was broad enough that trying to finish everything at once risked the timeline stretching indefinitely.
ResponseWe broke the work into the smallest viable units around the MVP, tested feature by feature with internal staff and target users first, then expanded to external testers before launch — spreading the risk out in stages.
03Sharply cut down the tutorial/pairing steps and the depth of detailed settings
RiskTrimming explanations risked leaving more users unable to use features simply because they didn't know they existed.
ResponseWe cut Bluetooth pairing from 6 steps to 3, and the tutorial from 14 steps to 3, replacing whatever we boldly cut with in-app coach marks. Detailed settings were pushed down a level, and we prioritized surfacing the control features users relied on most, based on GA and interview data.
