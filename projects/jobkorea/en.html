Problem Definition

The homepage wasn't working as an entry gate for first-time visitors

74.2% of all visitors arrived logged out, yet the existing homepage showed the same content (fixed ad tiers) no matter whether it had personalization data on the visitor — making it hard for people to find what they were actually looking for.

Evidence-Based Case

GA Analysis · Behavioral Data · Interviews

With 74.2% of visitors logged out, most had no personalization data on file, and mobile's bounce rate (23.6%) ran 2.4x higher than PC's (9.8%), while time on site was far shorter on mobile (1:18) than PC (2:28). The first screen was deciding everything.

Both entry-level and experienced users reach for search first, and demand for AI recommendations already ranks #2. The way things were displayed was the real problem.

"The homepage feels like a billboard"
"All I see are listings that have nothing to do with me"
"I've never scrolled all the way to the bottom"
"It had an AD tag on it, so I skipped it even when the role fit"
Key Insight: Users clearly intend to find listings through search (51–53%), and demand for AI recommendations is already there (34–36%). The problem is that, regardless of login status, only ad-tier listings surface without any personalization. The mismatch between what content users wanted and how it was displayed was driving the bounce.
Decisions · Trade-offs

4 To-be Components: Validated Through UT

Restructured Homepage Layout

Redesigned the information hierarchy and reordered sections to match what users came for.

Login Prompt Banner

Made clear that logging in unlocks more accurate recommendations, nudging a natural conversion.

Introduced Onboarding

Collects job function and experience level without requiring login, enabling personalization from the very first visit.

AI Recommendation Cards

Surfaces matched listings based on the collected info, applying an AI match score within the existing ad tiers.

To-be Flow: First visit → onboarding → AI matching → personalized homepage → login prompt → precision recommendations

Key Trade-off: Pure AI recommendations (risking advertiser revenue) vs. keeping ad tiers as the only content (continued user churn). This wasn't purely a UX call — we also had to weigh the ad products already being sold (our corporate clients' revenue). We kept the ad tier structure (First VVIP–Excellent) intact, but used an AI match score to prioritize exposure within each tier, adding a personalization section inside our existing business constraints.

Ad Tier Structure: First VVIP · VIP · Excellent

Structure: Only the sections with an image and an "AD" label are actual paid ad products — the AI Recommendations and Curated Jobs sections above them are plain organic listings.

Original Problem: Multiple ad products, priced higher the closer to the top, were listed with no visual distinction between them.

Improvement: Applied differentiated UI per product and gave personalized slots priority over ads at the top → cut ad product types from 5 down to 3. CPC products kept their interspersed placement within the list, but CPT (fixed-placement) ad space was cut significantly.

Light vs. Heavy Users: Common Ground and Differences

Common ground: Job hunting is a "have to do," so efficiency and time savings matter most. Users like AI recommendations, but trust drops without supporting reasons.

Light users: Quickly check only the listings they need right now, then stop. Anything that "looks like an ad" gets skipped instantly; scrolling is short and detail screens rarely get a deep look.

Heavy users: Since they've opened the app already, they try to squeeze out as much info as possible — moving through multiple sections and staying noticeably longer.

5 To-be Improvements Ready to Ship Immediately

01 Rewrite section titles: swap vague phrases like "Jobs You Don't Want to Miss" for ones that show the actual criteria — applicant count, closing soon, recently posted, etc.

02 Tone down the AD label: it stood out too sharply against the background and text, prompting "I skipped it even when the role fit" — so we dialed back the color

03 Change the onboarding banner title/UI: "Balance Game" read as "a game that'll eat up my time," causing people to close it immediately → A/B tested against "Get Matched Listings," confirming higher click intent and favorability

04 Surface AI Insight's reasoning: added text explaining the recommendation criteria at the section and card level, to answer the "why am I seeing this?" question

05 Rework personalized-service naming: renamed unclear labels like "Job Search TOOL" into names that draw more interest

Shipped before launch: Of these, the AD label tone adjustment and the onboarding banner UI change (removing the element that nudged users to close it) went in before the official release.

Homepage Structure

Scanning the homepage top to bottom, the existing search habit stays exactly where it was, with personalization-driven sections stacked underneath it, one after another.

Keeping the search habit, going deeper on personalization

We left the most-used habit — unified search (#1, at 51–53%) — untouched, and added region, the most-used filter, to give users one more starting point for browsing.

Today's AI Insight

UT surfaced feedback that "the AI feature is hard to trust," so we added copy explaining why each listing was recommended, aiming to build trust.

Listings matched by job function & experience

We used the job function and experience data collected during onboarding to power the AI Recommendations and Curated Jobs sections, so personalization data actually translated into real listings.

Kept the ad products, adjusted the label tone

Given the ad revenue structure, we kept the ad tier sections (VVIP through First), but toned down the overly prominent "AD" label to ease the problem of users skipping past listings even when the role fit.

Curated Jobs · Diversifying how users browse

Users can now filter instantly by region, job function, or major — narrowing down their search to the conditions they want, without relying on the search bar alone.

What We Learned

Trust came from "match accuracy," not from "explanation."

In a follow-up A/B test, whether or not the AI Insight copy was shown made no difference to clicks or applications — the variant without it actually converted slightly higher.

The "AI trust and label confusion" predicted in UT resurfaced almost exactly in post-launch VOC, confirming that our upfront research had accurately predicted real-world reactions.

Key Insight

Users didn't actually want the reasoning explained to them — if the result was right, they clicked without needing to know why.

→ The fix for the trust problem wasn't "explain more" — it was "strip away the explanation and prove it through match accuracy."

Follow-up · Second A/B Test AI Insight Follow-up Validation

Do we really need to "show the AI recommendation reasoning"? Testing the net effect of the copy itself

UT had surfaced a "lack of trust in AI Insight," leaving "improve how recommendation reasoning is shown" as a next step. But before figuring out how to display that reasoning better, we validated a more fundamental question first: what if the AI Insight copy and explanation area didn't need to exist at all? On the homepage's "Today's AI Recommendations" section, we A/B split whether the top Insight copy appeared at all and measured its incrementality.

Experiment Design

User-level random assignment · 50:50 traffic split
Mar 4–11, 2026 (1 week, weekdays and weekend)

Variants Compared

Variant A: Detailed AI Insight copy + recommendation list
Variant B: Concise label + recommendation list (Insight copy minimized)

Success Metrics

Listing clicks and applications per member (primary)
Homepage CTR/CVR (secondary)

Variant A · Detailed Insight Copy

Variant B · Final Adopted

Result: Raw click and application counts were similar between the two variants, and click-through/conversion rates actually came out slightly higher for Variant B, with its shorter Insight copy. The conclusion wasn't "how do we show the reasoning better" — it was "the long explanation was never needed."

Final Decision

We decided to remove the AI Insight copy area entirely and show only the recommendation list (AgentRec). The next step we'd planned — "improve recommendation reasoning" — ended up resolved not by polishing the copy, but by stripping out an explanation that never needed to be there in the first place. A case that confirmed trust problems aren't always solved by explaining more.
