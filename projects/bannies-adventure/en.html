Purpose · Hypothesis

A Game Born From the "Speech Banana" Research

Patients with hearing loss need speech-sound discrimination training, but existing training is just repetitive auditory drilling — hard to stick with, and drop-off was common. While researching papers and materials on speech-sound discrimination training, I came across "Speech Banana," a chart that maps words and sounds across frequency bands. I judged that applying this concept to a game could turn the training into something people would actually stay with.

If:

we turn the Speech Banana concept — mapping words and sounds to frequency bands — into game rules,

Then:

resistance to repetitive training will drop, and speech-sound discrimination will be trained naturally.

Speech Banana (Phoneme Bank)

It refers to the banana-shaped region on an audiogram where the phonemes of everyday speech fall, and it's a reference chart used in audiology and speech rehabilitation to explain which phonemes are audible or inaudible at different degrees of hearing loss. I used this chart as the basis for classifying every word used in the game.

Word Classification by Speech Banana (Words Table)

I classified the actual words used in the game by frequency band (low to high) and decibel range. Low frequencies (250–1000Hz) were split into zones A and B, and high frequencies (1000–8000Hz) into zones C, D, and E, with a matching word set mapped to each zone.

Decisions & Trade-offs

Two Mini-Games for Remembering and Finding Sound

Bannie's Cup

A game where players use their eyes and ears to remember and find the character saying the same word — training that links sound to both visual and auditory memory.

Bannie's Room

A game where players listen for a sound hidden somewhere in a room and locate its direction and position — training the ability to pick a target sound out of background noise.

We built a prototype game while checking game composition (scenario, design), difficulty, network communication, and data management.

Scoring Design by Stage and Question Type

Each stage randomly draws single or combined questions from the low- and high-frequency zones (based on the Words Table), and the score accumulates as a multiplier depending on how many answers are correct and whether they form a streak. We also collect game start/end time, correct answers versus attempts, and each player's percentile position within their age group.

01Design a game-first UI instead of a training-first one
RiskPrioritizing pure fun risks diluting the actual speech-sound discrimination training effect.
ResponseWe built the Speech Banana frequency/sound mapping directly into the game rules themselves — finding the character saying the same word, locating the object making a sound — so fun and the training objective were never separated.
02Support English, Japanese, and Korean simultaneously
RiskSince pronunciation and word sets differ by language, content and validation resources triple.
ResponseWe designed the game composition (scenario, difficulty) and data management system to be language-independent, so only the word set needed to swap per language.
03Communicate with the outsourced game studio through hand-drawn references
RiskA text-only spec risks failing to accurately convey the intended sense of space and mood to the studio.
ResponseI drew hand sketches in stages — layout, then props/color/mood, then specific objects — to clearly guide the direction of the actual 3D asset production.
Design Process

Guiding the Studio's Direction With Hand-Drawn References

I hand-drew sketches and references myself to communicate the design direction I wanted to the game development studio. (Example: Bannie's Room · Living Room)

From layout, to overall prop feel/color/mood, to specifying exactly which objects needed to appear — I set the direction with hand sketches first, then guided it through to the actual 3D assets.

Storyboard · The Banana in the Cup

From stage entry to character appearance, question prompts, and correct-answer reactions, I laid out Screen, Context, Audio, and Time cut by cut and handed it to the studio.

Outcomes

Live in Full Service Across English, Japanese, and Korean

Status: Both mini-games, Bannie's Cup and Bannie's Room, have been completed with real 3D assets and are now live in full service across three languages within Olive Union's DTx offering, alongside other tinnitus and hearing services like Olive Aid. No separate quantitative performance data was compiled, so it isn't reflected on this page.
